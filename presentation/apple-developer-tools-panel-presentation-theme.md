## What I Learned From The Designer Interview

### Product signals

- More people are becoming app builders, so App Store Connect may need to serve a broader range of users than experienced professional developers alone.
- The design problem is not simply making the existing interface easier. It is deciding how one platform should support people with different experience levels, responsibilities, and goals.
- App distribution is also shaped by changing country and regional compliance requirements. App Store Connect may need to absorb more rules and exceptions without making the core publishing workflow harder to understand.
- These are interview signals, not confirmed Apple roadmap commitments. Use them to frame the relevance of the work without presenting them as inside knowledge.

### Portfolio signal

- The designer asked to see non-chat interface work, particularly dashboards or similar product surfaces.
- The panel should therefore show that I am not only an AI or conversational-interface designer.
- I need clear evidence of information architecture, hierarchy, data visualization, role-based experiences, reusable systems, and actions embedded in complex product surfaces.

## Selected Theme

### One Platform, Many Users

**Designing complex systems that adapt to different roles, changing requirements, and the decisions people need to make.**

### First-principles thesis

> As a platform grows, the answer is not to make every user learn the entire system. The system should understand who is doing the work, make the right information and actions clear, and remain adaptable as requirements change.

### Why this theme fits the role

- App builders are not one audience. A first-time builder, an experienced developer, a business owner, and a compliance specialist may enter the same platform with different knowledge and responsibilities.
- App Store Connect must support those differences while preserving a coherent product model.
- Global requirements will continue to add states, dependencies, and exceptions. The design must make those rules actionable without exposing unnecessary complexity to everyone.
- This requires product judgment as well as interface craft: deciding what should be a shared default, what should adapt by role or context, and what must remain visible for trust and accountability.

## How The Projects Support The Theme

### CHAI — Make complex data understandable in context

- Administrators were trying to understand reports and dashboards, not looking for another chat destination.
- I helped shift the product from a separate assistant toward contextual help inside the workflows where decisions were already happening.
- The relevant panel evidence is the report-analysis experience, dashboard context, information hierarchy, visible scope, and the product decisions behind those patterns.
- This project demonstrates product framing, data-heavy interaction design, and how I moved beyond a top-down request to define a broader product direction.

### SAP Fieldglass — Design strong defaults for different roles

- Hiring managers were important users, but many did not work in Fieldglass every day. They needed the next action to be clear without becoming power users.
- I mapped four core personas — Hiring Manager, PMO, Financial Approver, and System Admin — to their most important jobs.
- I proposed a role-based dashboard direction instead of treating the work as a visual refresh of individual widgets.
- The resulting system used modular SAP UI5 cards, best-practice role templates, configurable layouts, surfaced work items, and in-place actions.
- The redesigned homepage framework was adopted by more than 1,000 enterprise customers.
- This project demonstrates non-chat interface craft, user segmentation, system thinking, product strategy, and designing a scalable balance between defaults and configuration.

## The Connection Between The Projects

The two projects should not feel like unrelated case studies.

- SAP asks: **How can one platform give different roles the right starting point?**
- CHAI asks: **How can the platform help people understand complex information at the moment they need to decide?**
- Together they show: **I design systems that meet people where they are while preserving the depth, flexibility, and accountability of the platform.**

## What The Panel Should Learn About Me

- I can design dense dashboards and enterprise workflows, not only conversational AI.
- I segment users by their jobs, responsibilities, and decision needs rather than relying on broad labels such as beginner and expert.
- I can turn a narrow request into a stronger product model when research points to a more important problem.
- I make product and strategy decisions in partnership with PM and engineering; I do not wait for a finished brief or execute a queue of tickets.
- I look for the reusable system behind an individual screen.
- I care about clear defaults, progressive complexity, visible state, and actions placed where the relevant context already exists.
- I can explain what I personally decided, what evidence shaped the decision, and how the work changed through collaboration.

## Guardrails For The Future Outline

- Do not frame the portfolio as “AI plus an older dashboard project.” Frame both projects as evidence of the same product-design philosophy.
- Do not over-index on chat screens in CHAI. Lead with the report or dashboard, the user decision, and the contextual interaction.
- Do not claim direct experience designing international app-store compliance workflows. Use SAP to demonstrate the transferable ability to organize roles, requirements, states, and exceptions.
- Do not describe different app builders only as novice versus expert. Consider responsibilities, frequency of use, team structure, permissions, and the consequence of mistakes.
- Keep shipped and measured work separate from directional concepts.
- Show the dashboard system at multiple levels: the overall information model, one or two key interactions, and the visual details that make dense information scannable.
