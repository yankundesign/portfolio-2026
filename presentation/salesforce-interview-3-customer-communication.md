# Salesforce Interview 3 — Customer Success and Effective Communication

**Interviewers:** Senior Director, Product Management (Hiring Manager); Senior Director, UI/UX  
**Competencies:** Champion Customer Success; Courageous Communicator — Communicates Effectively

## What the Panel Is Likely Testing

- Do I begin with the customer's real workflow, not the requested feature?
- Can I identify and remove barriers that affect adoption or task success?
- Do I follow through with evidence that the solution worked?
- Can I communicate clearly with customers, executives, PMs, designers, and engineers?
- Can I listen, adapt my language, and make complex AI decisions understandable?
- Can I be direct about risk, uncertainty, and product limitations?

## Core Message

> I advocate for customers by understanding the work behind the request, then translating that understanding into priorities the team can act on. I adjust the depth and format of my communication, but I keep the decision and its consequences clear.

## Story Bank

- **SAP Fieldglass homepage:** Difficult-to-see customer expectations; role-based defaults; 1,000+ enterprise customers.
- **Smart Search:** Removed a barrier at a known point of failure; 86% fewer dead-end searches.
- **CHAI contextual AI:** Customer behavior changed the product direction; 3% → 18% monthly adoption.
- **Control Hub Agentic prototype:** Adjusted communication across executives, product, design, and engineering.

## Q&A

### 1. Tell me about a time customer expectations were initially difficult to identify.

- **Testing:** Actively seeking to understand the customer's circumstances and needs.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "At SAP Fieldglass, the initial requests were to update homepage widgets and improve Global Search."
  - **Task:** "I needed to understand what occasional users like hiring managers actually expected when they entered the product."
  - **Action:** "Research showed that they did not want to become expert search users. They wanted the product to surface the right task, worker signal, or decision without making them hunt."
  - **Action:** "I mapped four core personas to their top jobs and translated those needs into role-based dashboard templates and in-place actions."
  - **Result:** "The redesigned homepage launched as a modular framework and was adopted by more than 1,000 enterprise customers."
  - **Learning:** "The customer's request may name a feature, but the expectation is often a better outcome."

### 2. Tell me about a time you identified and removed a barrier to customer success.

- **Testing:** Recognizing a service barrier and taking customer-focused action.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "Control Hub admins often used search because they did not know where a setting lived, but failed queries ended with no useful next step."
  - **Task:** "I needed to reduce that dead end and help admins continue toward an answer."
  - **Action:** "I designed Smart Search to accept natural-language queries, return the most relevant setting with context, and offer follow-up questions that opened CHAI without losing the search context."
  - **Action:** "I iterated using query-log behavior and kept the handoff attached to the admin's original intent."
  - **Result:** "The no-result rate dropped by 86%, and search became an entry point into deeper assistance instead of a stopping point."
  - **Closer:** "The improvement was not only better retrieval; it was preserving momentum after the search."

### 3. How did you check whether CHAI was actually meeting customer needs?

- **Testing:** Following up with outcome measures, not assuming success after launch.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I looked at both behavior and the quality of the workflow."
  - "Adoption told us whether admins returned, while search success and entry points showed where CHAI was creating value."
  - "Research sessions helped explain the behavior—for example, why admins needed answers grounded in the report or device already on screen."
  - "That combination changed the roadmap from improving a destination to integrating AI into search, reports, dashboards, and troubleshooting."
  - "Monthly adoption moved from 3% to 18%, but the more meaningful signal was that usage became attached to real work."

### 4. Tell me about a time customer feedback changed your product direction.

- **Testing:** Listening, prioritizing the customer's perspective, and adjusting behavior.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "CHAI could answer help questions, but admins were not building a habit around it."
  - **Task:** "I needed to understand why a technically working assistant was not useful enough to revisit."
  - **Action:** "In research, I listened for the work around the question: failed searches, delivered reports, confusing dashboards, and device issues."
  - **Action:** "I reframed the roadmap around Find, Understand, and Fix, then designed contextual entry points that carried the relevant data into CHAI."
  - **Result:** "The assistant became a layer of help inside admin workflows, and monthly adoption grew from 3% to 18%."
  - **Learning:** "Customers did not ask for more AI. They needed less work between the problem and useful help."

### 5. Give an example of adjusting your communication style to the situation.

- **Testing:** Communicating to the listener's background and expectations.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "The agentic Control Hub project needed alignment from executives, PM, design, and engineering, but each group needed a different level of detail."
  - **Task:** "I needed to keep one product direction coherent without giving every audience the same presentation."
  - **Action:** "With executives, I led with the customer risk and the decision: AI must plan visibly before it acts."
  - **Action:** "With PM and design, I used end-to-end workflows and tradeoffs; with engineering, I focused on state, permissions, dependencies, and failure handling."
  - **Action:** "The working prototype gave everyone a shared artifact while letting me change the depth of the conversation."
  - **Result:** "The group aligned around a concrete model and supported moving the direction toward a Cisco Live US 2026 demo."

### 6. Describe a time you had to keep a group engaged while presenting complex information.

- **Testing:** Organization, attention, and clear narrative.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "Agentic AI created a long list of topics—chat, workflows, permissions, approvals, execution, audit, and rollback."
  - **Task:** "I needed leadership to evaluate the product direction without getting lost in a framework tour."
  - **Action:** "I organized the story around one question: what does the admin need to see before safely saying yes?"
  - **Action:** "I used one end-to-end device onboarding flow to show intent, missing inputs, plan, approval, execution, and Activity."
  - **Action:** "I paused at the decision moments and used the prototype to invite reactions instead of presenting every screen."
  - **Result:** "The discussion became specific to trust and product behavior rather than staying at the level of general AI opinions."

### 7. Tell me about creating space for others so you could understand their perspective.

- **Testing:** Active listening and openness.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "In the agentic project, some stakeholders liked the value of automation but worried that an AI-first experience could stop feeling like Control Hub."
  - **Task:** "I needed to understand whether the resistance was to AI itself or to losing a familiar and controllable home base."
  - **Action:** "Instead of defending the concept, I asked where they felt disoriented, what information they expected to remain visible, and which actions felt too consequential."
  - **Action:** "I summarized the concern back as a design principle: the agent should work inside Control Hub, not replace it."
  - **Result:** "That feedback shaped a more familiar surface, progressive evidence, plan review, and Activity as the persistent record."
  - **Closer:** "Listening changed the model, not just the wording of the presentation."

### 8. Tell me about communicating a difficult truth or limitation.

- **Testing:** Clarity, courage, and trust.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "In the agentic project, there was pressure to make the AI feel fast and capable."
  - **Task:** "I needed to explain that immediate execution would create the wrong kind of product confidence for high-stakes admin work."
  - **Action:** "I made the consequences concrete: hidden dependencies, incorrect scope, permission failures, and changes that might not be reversible."
  - **Action:** "I proposed a plan-first interaction where speed could increase for low-risk work, but consequential actions required evidence and explicit approval."
  - **Result:** "The team aligned around bounded action and reviewable execution instead of treating autonomy as the primary success measure."
  - **Closer:** "My message was that trust is not friction we should automatically remove."

### 9. How do you communicate when product and design disagree?

- **Testing:** Directness without damaging trust.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I state the disagreement clearly and connect it to the shared outcome."
  - "I ask questions first so I understand the product, timing, or technical constraint behind the other view."
  - "Then I show the alternatives, evidence, and consequence for the customer instead of arguing from taste."
  - "If the decision is reversible, I look for a scoped way to test it."
  - "Once a decision is made, I document it, support it, and define what evidence would cause us to revisit it."

### 10. How do you explain a complex AI system to a non-technical audience?

- **Testing:** Audience awareness and clarity.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I start with the user's decision, not the model architecture."
  - "For CHAI, I would say the assistant becomes useful when it knows which report, dashboard, search, or device the admin is already working with."
  - "For agentic AI, I use a simple contract: the user states the goal, the system shows its plan, the user approves it, and the product records what happened."
  - "I add technical depth only when it changes the decision, such as permissions, grounding, failure states, or rollback."
  - "If the listener cannot explain the decision back, I have probably included too much system language."

### 11. How would you balance one customer's request with a scalable platform need?

- **Testing:** Customer advocacy with enterprise product judgment.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I first separate the requested solution from the underlying job and constraint."
  - "Then I look for evidence that the need repeats across roles, workflows, or customers."
  - "At SAP, that led from individual homepage requests to role-based templates and reusable UI5 cards."
  - "The default handled common needs, while configuration gave customers room for real differences."
  - "That balance helped the framework scale to more than 1,000 enterprise customers."

### 12. Why Salesforce and why this role?

- **Testing:** Motivation, fit, and executive-level clarity.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "My strongest work has been in complex enterprise products where users need to understand data, make consequential decisions, and trust AI inside existing workflows."
  - "Salesforce operates at a scale where design decisions have to work across products, roles, and customer contexts—not just one feature."
  - "This role is attractive because it combines platform thinking, customer impact, and close partnership with product and engineering."
  - "I see it as a chance to bring my enterprise AI experience into a broader ecosystem and take on more cross-product ownership."

### 13. How do you conduct research with highly technical users?

- **Testing:** Customer understanding without relying on surface-level requests.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I ask technical users to walk through a recent real case rather than describe their ideal process in the abstract."
  - "I pay attention to the artifacts they use—logs, dashboards, reports, scripts, support cases, and handoff notes—because those reveal where context is lost."
  - "For Control Hub troubleshooting, I worked with research and Cisco Technical Assistance Center experts to understand how they establish scope, compare signals, and decide what to investigate next."
  - "I turn those observations into a workflow or system model, then validate it with the experts before treating it as a design foundation."
  - "The goal is to capture how they reason, not only the features they request."

### 14. How do you validate a design for technical users such as developers?

- **Testing:** Evaluation rigor and understanding of expert behavior.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I test with realistic tasks, data, terminology, and failure conditions because a simplified demo can hide the hardest usability problems."
  - "I include users with different levels of familiarity so I can distinguish onboarding problems from expert-efficiency problems."
  - "I look at whether users can predict what the system will do, interpret its state, recover from an error, and verify the result."
  - "For a developer-facing workflow, I would also test how the experience fits with existing tools, documentation, APIs, and debugging practices."
  - "Success is not only completing the happy path; it is being able to understand and recover when the system behaves unexpectedly."

### 15. How do you design one technical product for both occasional and expert users?

- **Testing:** Inclusive product strategy and scalable interaction design.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I start by identifying what both groups must understand, then where their needs separate."
  - "Occasional users usually need stronger defaults, guidance, and clearer next actions; experts need speed, density, and access to underlying detail."
  - "At SAP Fieldglass, role-based templates gave occasional users a strong starting point while configuration supported different enterprise needs."
  - "In Control Hub, contextual entry points reduced orientation work, while detailed evidence and audit records remained available for deeper investigation."
  - "I use progressive disclosure and multiple entry points rather than forcing every user through the same amount of explanation."

### 16. How do you communicate a complex technical design to PM, engineering, and leadership?

- **Testing:** Audience-aware communication and cross-functional alignment.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I keep the core product decision consistent, but change the depth and artifact for the audience."
  - "With leadership, I lead with the customer risk, product principle, and decision required."
  - "With PM and design, I show the end-to-end workflow, alternatives, and customer tradeoffs."
  - "With engineering, I focus on state, data, permissions, dependencies, failure handling, and what the prototype assumes."
  - "A shared working prototype helps everyone discuss the same behavior instead of interpreting separate documents differently."

### 17. How do you design accessible, data-rich technical interfaces?

- **Testing:** Accessibility as part of core interaction quality.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I treat accessibility as part of the interaction model, not a final compliance check."
  - "For data-rich interfaces, I use clear hierarchy, meaningful labels, predictable focus order, keyboard access, and state indicators that do not depend on color alone."
  - "For tables, charts, logs, and execution states, I make sure the important meaning is available in text and that status changes are communicated clearly."
  - "I also test zoom, text expansion, loading, empty, error, and long-content states because technical data rarely stays within the ideal layout."
  - "The goal is for every user to understand system state and take the next action confidently."

### 18. Tell me about a time when you established trust with someone despite having differing viewpoints.

- **Testing:** Integrity, empathy, openness, and the ability to find common ground.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "In the agentic Control Hub project, I proposed a detailed Activity experience for agent actions, while the PM wanted to reuse the existing Control Hub audit log with its more limited information."
  - **Task:** "I needed to advocate for the transparency admins required without dismissing the PM's concerns about platform consistency, scope, and implementation cost."
  - **Action:** "I first made sure I understood the PM's position and acknowledged that creating a parallel audit system could fragment the product and increase engineering effort."
  - **Action:** "I shared research showing that admins needed more detail to trust agent work, and I partnered with engineering to identify the steps, approvals, affected resources, and outcomes we could realistically capture."
  - **Action:** "I proposed extending the existing audit-log structure with a new event type and richer agent-action details instead of replacing it."
  - **Result:** "We both adjusted our positions, reached a solution that met the customer and platform needs, and built more trust because the decision incorporated each person's concerns."
- **Closer:** "I built trust by showing that I was committed to the shared outcome, not to defending my original solution."

### 19. Describe a time when you had to objectively consider ideas that conflicted with your own.

- **Testing:** Openness, self-awareness, and willingness to change direction.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "In the early agentic exploration, I was interested in giving AI a stronger, more central presence in Control Hub."
  - **Task:** "Stakeholders and users challenged that direction because they worried the experience would stop feeling like the Control Hub they already understood and trusted."
  - **Action:** "Instead of defending the concept, I asked where they felt disoriented, what product context needed to remain visible, and which parts of the existing experience still mattered to their work."
  - **Action:** "I reviewed the feedback against the product goal and realized their concern was valid: an agent could be more capable without becoming a separate center of gravity."
  - **Action:** "I adjusted the direction so the agent worked inside the familiar Control Hub structure, with deeper evidence and agent details appearing progressively when users needed them."
  - **Result:** "The concept became more useful and less theatrical, and the clearer direction helped build stakeholder alignment for the Cisco Live US 2026 demo."
- **Closer:** "Objectively considering the conflicting view improved the product because it helped me separate the value of the idea from the form I first gave it."

## Questions to Ask the Panel

### For the Senior Director, Product Management

- "What customer problem is most important for this role to improve in the next six months?"
- "Where do customer requests currently conflict most with the need for a scalable platform direction?"
- "How do PM and design share responsibility for customer discovery and outcome measurement?"

### For the Senior Director, UI/UX

- "What communication behaviors distinguish the designers who have the most influence here?"
- "How does design maintain a coherent experience when teams and product areas have different priorities?"
- "Where would you want this person to raise the quality bar first: product framing, interaction systems, craft, or cross-functional process?"

### For Both

- "What would you need to see in the first six months to feel confident that this hire is improving both customer outcomes and team effectiveness?"
- "When product, customer evidence, and technical constraints point in different directions, how does this leadership team make the decision?"

## More Questions About the Team, Culture, and Management

Choose two or three based on what the conversation has not already answered.

### Team Structure

- "How is the design team structured, and how would this role work with designers supporting adjacent product areas?"

- "What behaviors tend to earn trust and influence on this team?"
- "Can you share a recent example of a healthy disagreement and how the team worked through it?"

- "How would you describe your management style, especially the balance between giving direction and giving designers autonomy?"
