## OpenArt In Plain English

The simple user workflow:

1. A person has a story, character, campaign, scene, or visual idea.
2. OpenArt helps shape that idea into a usable creative direction.
3. The system generates images, video, voices, audio, characters, and worlds.
4. The creator gives feedback and changes specific parts.
5. The product preserves continuity across scenes, assets, style, and story.
6. The creator leaves with a finished artifact and returns to continue the project.

The strategic shift is:

```text
Generate an asset
-> develop a character or world
-> direct a sequence
-> refine a coherent story
-> build a reusable creative identity
```

OpenArt's public story says consistent characters were a major signal that users were not merely making images; they were trying to tell stories. The company describes its evolution from an image tool into a platform spanning image, video, characters, audio, and longer-form storytelling.

Director makes that strategy concrete:

- Begin with a half-formed idea or creative vision.
- Develop story, character, pacing, references, and visual style through conversation.
- Generate a longer, coherent work rather than disconnected short clips.
- Refine individual scenes and frames.
- Preserve character, environment, product, voice, and brand consistency.
- Keep the person in the creative-director role while the AI handles production complexity.

## Top Product Pain Points

### 1. A creator's intent is richer than a prompt

- People think in story, mood, pacing, references, character motivation, brand, and audience.
- A blank prompt asks them to translate all of that into system language before they can start.
- The interface needs to help form the brief without taking authorship away from the creator.

### 2. Powerful generation does not guarantee a finished outcome

- One strong image or clip is not a complete film, ad, campaign, or story.
- Users need sequencing, continuity, revision, project memory, and publishing workflows.
- The product needs to preserve the connection between intent, generated assets, and the finished artifact.

### 3. Creative control is both conversational and direct

- Conversation is approachable for high-level direction.
- Precise work often needs selection, timeline or scene structure, explicit values, comparison, and direct manipulation.
- The design challenge is deciding when to use language, when to expose structure, and how changes stay synchronized.

### 4. Probabilistic systems create costly iteration

- Generation takes time and may consume credits or limited resources.
- Results can be almost right but fail in one important region, frame, character, or transition.
- Users need clear progress, cancellability, versioning, targeted regeneration, recovery, and confidence that a local edit will not destroy the rest.

### 5. Consistency must survive across time

- Characters, objects, environments, voices, products, and brand details need to persist across scenes and projects.
- The product must communicate what the system remembers and what needs reinforcement.
- A failure of consistency is not only a model issue; it can break the creator's mental model of the whole project.

### 6. The product serves different levels of creative expertise

- A first-time creator needs a useful starting path and language they understand.
- A professional needs control over pacing, framing, continuity, assets, and delivery requirements.
- The strongest answer is likely progressive control, not separate "simple" and "professional" products.

### 7. Product breadth can become navigation complexity

- OpenArt currently exposes agents or modes, task-specific tools, models, projects, characters, worlds, brand assets, templates, and inspiration.
- Each is useful, but users still need to understand where to begin and how the pieces relate.
- The product architecture should follow the creator's job, not require understanding OpenArt's internal capability map.

## Recommended Deep-Dive Story

### Theme

- "From a separate assistant to contextual and agentic workflows."

### Why this is the strongest choice

- CHAI gives a shipped outcome and product judgment.
- Agentic shows forward-looking AI architecture, control, and functional prototyping.
- Together they show how the interaction model changed as AI moved from answering to helping complete work.
- The OpenArt connection is clear: a conversational entry is useful, but the product must preserve context, structure the work, support iteration, and keep the person in control.

### Six-to-eight-minute structure

#### 1. User job and friction - 60 seconds

- Administrators needed to find information, understand operational data, and resolve issues.
- The original assistant could answer questions, but it lived as a separate destination.
- Users had to leave the workflow and reconstruct context that Control Hub already knew.

#### 2. Product decision - 90 seconds

- I changed the model from "go ask the bot" to contextual assistance inside reports, dashboards, search, and device workflows.
- The location of the entry point supplied context and reduced prompt burden.
- The output needed to be structured around the user's decision, not only presented as prose.

#### 3. Shipped outcome - 30 seconds

- Broader monthly adoption moved from 3% to 18%.
- The lesson was that AI became useful when it understood where the user was and what they were trying to do.

#### 4. The next product question - 30 seconds

- Once AI could explain work, the next question was how it should help complete work.
- A natural-language request was not enough to authorize consequential action.

#### 5. Agentic interaction contract - 2 minutes

- I organized the experience around intent, context, plan, approval, execution, and Activity.
- The plan made assumptions, dependencies, scope, and important choices reviewable.
- Approval happened at a meaningful boundary.
- Execution remained visible, with failure and recovery states.
- Activity preserved what was requested, approved, attempted, and changed.

#### 6. Why I built it - 60 seconds

- Static screens hid timing, persistence, handoffs, interruption, and state changes.
- I built a working React and TypeScript prototype using AI coding tools.
- Product, design, and engineering could inspect the behavior and make tradeoffs together.

#### 7. OpenArt connection - 45 seconds

- "The domain is different, but the interaction lesson transfers. Conversation is a low-threshold way to express intent. The product still needs to preserve context, make the evolving artifact visible, support targeted correction, and give the person meaningful control. In OpenArt, that control serves creative agency rather than administrative accountability."

## Product Judgment For OpenArt

### What makes a strong AI creative product?

- It helps a person begin without requiring prompt expertise.
- It preserves the person's intent across iterations.
- It makes the evolving story or artifact visible, not only the conversation.
- It supports local correction without forcing a complete restart.
- It uses direct manipulation when language becomes slow or ambiguous.
- It communicates generation progress, cost, failure, and recovery clearly.
- It maintains consistency across characters, worlds, products, style, and voice.
- It remembers enough to support continuity without making memory feel mysterious.
- It helps the person finish, export, publish, and return.
- It amplifies creative judgment rather than replacing it.

### How to balance accessibility with professional control

Use progressive control:

```text
Express the idea naturally
-> receive a useful first structure
-> inspect the scenes, assets, and assumptions
-> refine through conversation or direct controls
-> compare versions
-> finish and publish
```

- Hide technical model syntax when the system can choose well.
- Expose meaningful creative decisions such as pacing, framing, continuity, timing, reference strength, and brand fidelity.
- Let experts override defaults without forcing beginners to configure everything first.
- Organize around the creative job, not the underlying model inventory.

### How I would approach a product critique

Do not walk into the interview with a redesign.

Say:

- "I would not want to prescribe a feature without understanding the funnel and creator evidence."
- "From the public product, the question I would investigate is the transition from a first successful generation into an ongoing project."
- "OpenArt has conversational modes, task-specific tools, projects, characters, worlds, and brand assets. I would want to understand where creators lose the thread between those pieces."
- "I would look at where people retry, abandon, switch modes, rebuild context, or fail to return to a project."
- "Then I would prototype the smallest high-friction handoff rather than redesign the whole workspace."

### Metrics I would want to understand

- Time to first accepted or saved output.
- Percentage of users who reach a completed/exported artifact.
- Number and quality of revisions before acceptance.
- Targeted edit success versus full regeneration.
- Project return rate across days or weeks.
- Character, world, or brand reuse across creations.
- Creator retention by job type.
- Conversion from one-off generation to ongoing project work.
- Failure, cancellation, and abandonment by generation stage.
- Qualitative quality: did the result match the creator's intent, and did they still feel authorship?
