## The Product In Plain English

App Store Connect is not one workflow. It is the operating system around an app's life on the App Store: people and roles, app records, builds, beta testing, submissions, review status, releases, analytics, sales, agreements, and payments.

TestFlight adds another connected workflow: define what to test, upload a build, organize internal or external testers, manage review and build eligibility, collect feedback and crash information, and decide when the build is ready to move forward.

The design challenge is therefore not just making forms look clean. It is helping a team understand:

- What state is the app or build in?
- What is blocking the next step?
- Who can take the action?
- What will change after the action?
- Which platform, version, group, territory, or role is in scope?
- What feedback or data should influence the next release?

# Section 2 — Portfolio Presentation And How To Sound Natural

## The Main Presentation Goal

The interviewer should leave with three impressions:

1. You can turn a complex technical workflow into a clear product model.
2. You can explain and defend subtle interaction decisions with evidence and tradeoffs.
3. You understand implementation closely enough to make the design real.

The projects divide those signals cleanly:

| Project | What it proves | Status boundary |
|---|---|---|
| CHAI Report Analysis | Shipped enterprise workflow, research-to-roadmap decision, data and interaction design, measurable broader outcome | Contextual CHAI work shipped; do not attribute the full adoption change to Report Analysis alone |
| Control Hub Agentic ending | 0-to-1 systems thinking, plan and approval contract, prototyping, future-facing product judgment | Directional working prototype; no shipped adoption metric |
| Ebb | Native interaction craft, technical constraint as product shape, restraint, accessibility thinking, hands-on building | Working personal iOS build; public release is next; no TestFlight or App Store submission claim yet |

## Why You Sound Scripted

The problem is not that you prepared too much. It is that the script is carrying three jobs at once:

- Remember what happened.
- Decide what matters.
- Find the next sentence.

Move the first two jobs into the structure before the interview. Then, while presenting, you only need to explain the current visual and land one decision.

## The New Speaking Model

For each frame, prepare four small pieces:

1. **Claim:** The first sentence. Memorize this.
2. **Orient:** Tell them what they are looking at.
3. **Decision:** Explain what you chose and why.
4. **Land:** State the consequence or tradeoff, then pause.

Example:

- **Claim:** “The first version could reliably understand only one report.”
- **Orient:** “On the left is the full Reports page; on the right is the row action opening CHAI with that report attached.”
- **Decision:** “I put the AI action on the row because the location made the technical scope visible.”
- **Land:** “It was honest and contextual, but it was also easy to miss.”

That is enough. You do not need a polished paragraph between each line.

## Rules For Natural Delivery

- Memorize only the first sentence and final landing line of each frame.
- Lead with the answer. Use: `I decided X because Y. The tradeoff was Z.`
- Give context only after the interviewer knows why it matters.
- Look at the visual before speaking. Describe the thing you are pointing at.
- Use your natural connectors: “so,” “with that,” “the next question was,” and “what changed here was.”
- Keep one sentence to one job.
- Name the problem before the solution.
- Say “I” for your decision and “we” for shared team work.
- Avoid “we wanted to” when you can name the actual owner, evidence, or constraint.
- Credit the researcher, PM, engineering lead, data scientist, and domain experts at the moment their contribution matters.
- After a decision and tradeoff, stop. Silence gives the interviewer room to enter.
- If interrupted, answer the question directly. Do not rush back to finish every prepared line.

## Project One — CHAI Report Analysis, About 8 Minutes

### What The Apple Designer Should Remember

> I took a broad platform goal—bring AI into Control Hub—and helped define where it belonged, what administrator problem it should solve, how its scope should be communicated, and how the team could ship it responsibly.

### Frame 1 — A Platform Direction, Not A Ticket — 45 seconds

**Claim to memorize:**

> The mandate was to bring AI into Control Hub; the product model was not already defined.

**Visual:**

- `Platform direction: bring AI into administration` -> `Product questions I drove` -> `Shipped contextual workflows`.
- Keep the team diagram small: you, PM, engineering lead, researcher, engineers, data scientist.

**Say:**

- “Control Hub is where enterprise administrators manage users, devices, services, and the data behind them.”
- “The company wanted to bring AI into that platform. That was the direction; it was not yet a product definition.”
- “I led product and interaction design. The PM shaped priorities and business context. Engineering and data science shaped feasibility. I framed which administrator problems mattered, where AI belonged, and what we could honestly promise.”

**Land:**

> I was designing the product definition with the team, not decorating a predetermined feature.

### Frame 2 — Low Adoption Changed The Product Strategy — 1 minute 15 seconds

**Claim to memorize:**

> CHAI launched as a separate assistant, but adoption was around 3%, so I questioned the product model rather than polishing the chat.

**Visual:**

- Left: separate assistant plus `~3% adoption`.
- Right: research-informed opportunity map—`Find -> Understand -> Fix`.
- Arrow between them: `Research changed the roadmap`.

**Say:**

- “At roughly 3% adoption, my first question was not how to polish the chat. It was why an administrator should leave the task, open an assistant, and invent a prompt.”
- “I framed that question, partnered with our researcher, and joined customer conversations.”
- “Across different administrator roles, three jobs repeated: find information, understand data, and fix issues.”
- “I proposed moving AI into those workflows instead of treating chat as the destination, then aligned the direction with PM and engineering.”

**Land:**

> The research did not validate a ticket. It changed what we believed the product should be.

### Frame 3 — The Report Already Had Data, Not An Explanation — 50 seconds

**Claim to memorize:**

> Administrators could see the numbers, but they still had to turn those numbers into an explanation and a decision.

**Visual:**

- One report with the manual work beside it: `find change -> verify scope -> interpret -> explain`.

**Say:**

- “The report already showed the data. The missing work was interpretation.”
- “The administrator still had to identify the meaningful change, verify the time range and filters, and explain what it meant.”
- “So the answer needed to stay attached to the report. Moving it elsewhere would make the user rebuild context and weaken trust.”

**Land:**

> The product problem was preserving the connection between the question, the scope, and the source.

### Frame 4 — Context Became The Product Contract — 1 minute 15 seconds

**Claim to memorize:**

> The placement of the AI control had to tell the truth about what the system understood.

**Visual:**

- Four compact alternatives: global assistant, page action, persistent panel, report-row action.
- Enlarge the selected report-row pattern and show the later page-level evolution as one small arrow.

**Say:**

- “The first version could reliably understand one report. So the control's scope needed to match the system's scope.”
- “A global entry, page action, or persistent panel suggested broader understanding than we actually had.”
- “I chose the report-row action because it attached the report automatically and made the boundary clear. I accepted that it would be easier to miss.”
- “When the capability expanded, I moved the entry to page level. The placement changed; the rule did not: always show the user what data is in scope.”

**Land:**

> This was a product-scope decision expressed through interaction design, not simply a button-placement decision.

### Frame 5 — Keep The Report Authoritative — 1 minute 10 seconds

**Claim to memorize:**

> I did not want CHAI to create a second competing version of the data.

**Visual:**

- Report and assistant together, annotated with report name, time range, filters, source evidence, and inferred interpretation.

**Say:**

- “My first principle was that AI should not become a competing source of truth.”
- “The report stayed visible, and the answer preserved the report name, time range, filters, and scope.”
- “I also distinguished source data from interpretation. The fuller provenance and correction model is how I would extend the shipped work today.”

It found fewer attempted openings and more intentional behavior over time, alongside periodic breaks and declining dismissal rates immediately after first use and after returning from breaks.
- Do not say the CHI study proved that the overall intervention “became wallpaper” or stopped working. Say that repetition created a design risk you wanted Ebb to explore.
- Do not present Ebb's anti-habituation approach as validated yet. The changing trace and loop response are product hypotheses for longer-term testing.

## How To Rehearse Without Memorizing A Script

### Pass 1 — Claims Only

- Put the deck in presentation mode.
- Say only the first sentence of every frame.
- If the story still makes sense, the spine is working.
- If two claims do the same job, merge or cut one.

### Pass 2 — Visual First

- For each frame, point to one thing before giving interpretation.
- Use this pattern: “What you are looking at is…” -> “I chose this because…” -> “The tradeoff was…”
- Do not speak for more than 45 seconds without referring to something visible.

### Pass 3 — Product Ownership

For every CHAI and Agentic frame, answer these five prompts without looking at the script:

1. What direction or constraint was given to me?
2. What product question did I frame?
3. What decision did I personally advocate for?
4. What evidence and partners changed or strengthened it?
5. What changed in the product or roadmap afterward?

If the answer sounds like “the PM asked, then I designed,” rewrite it. If it sounds like you made every decision alone, add the partner and constraint back. The target is independent judgment through collaboration.

### Pass 4 — Timed, With Cuts

- Target about 8 minutes for Report Analysis, 2 for Agentic, and 2 for Ebb.
- Mark the slides you can cut without breaking the thesis.
- Practice the short version in the same session as the full version.
- Do not solve timing by speaking faster.

### Pass 5 — Interruptions

Ask a friend, or pause yourself at random, with:

- “What did you personally own?”
- “What alternative did you reject?”
- “What did engineering change?”
- “How do you know that worked?”
- “Was this shipped?”
- “What would you do differently?”
- “What happened in an error state?”
- “How did critique change the design?”

Answer in three parts:

1. Direct answer in one sentence.
2. Two pieces of evidence.
3. Honest caveat or tradeoff.

Then say:

> I can keep going from here, or go back to the walkthrough.

## Cue Card Format

## Recovery Lines When You Lose Your Place

- “The decision I want to land here is…”
- “What changed because of this was…”
- “The constraint that mattered was…”
- “The tradeoff was…”
- “Let me connect this back to the user.”
- “The next question we had to answer was…”

These sound natural because they return to the reasoning, not the memorized sentence.

## Presentation Language To Keep

- “So…”
- “With that…”
- “What changed here was…”
- “The next question was…”
- “I took a step back…”
- “I would not attribute that result to this feature alone.”
- “This part is directional.”
- “The tradeoff was…”

## Presentation Language To Remove

- “As you can see…”
- “This slide shows…”
- “We leveraged…”
- “A seamless end-to-end experience…”
- “Users loved it…” without evidence
- “Obviously…”
- “The AI decided…” when you mean the team defined the behavior
- Long lists of process phases that do not change the decision
