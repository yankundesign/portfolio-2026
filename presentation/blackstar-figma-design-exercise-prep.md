# Blackstar — 1-Hour Figma Design Exercise

## What I want them to remember

- I find the important problem before drawing the interface.
- I can turn ambiguity into a clear product direction without waiting for perfect information.
- I think across the complete experience: person, context, system behavior, interface, control, and recovery.
- I make deliberate tradeoffs and move quickly without becoming careless.
- I am collaborative, receptive, and comfortable changing direction when new information appears.
- I can bring one important interaction to a convincing level of craft.

## The central mindset

Treat the exercise as a collaborative product-thinking session. Figma is the shared surface, not the test itself.

The goal is not to produce the most screens. The goal is to demonstrate how I:

1. Understand an unfamiliar problem.
2. Decide what matters.
3. Form and communicate a point of view.
4. Translate that point of view into coherent product behavior.
5. Respond to constraints and feedback.
6. Finish with a clear, testable direction.

## Behavioral principles

### 1. Be curious before being clever

- Understand the person, situation, and desired outcome before proposing features.
- Ask questions that could change the design direction.
- Do not use questions to delay making a decision.

**Say:**

“Let me make sure I’m solving the right problem. The person is trying to ___, but today ___ gets in the way. Is that the central problem you want me to explore?”

### 2. Clarify enough, then move

- Separate facts from assumptions.
- State important assumptions visibly in the file.
- Choose a reasonable assumption and continue when information is unavailable.
- Explain what would change if the assumption proved false.

**Say:**

“We don’t know ___, so for this exercise I’ll assume ___. If that changes, I would reconsider ___.”

### 3. Think aloud through decisions, not cursor movements

- Explain why a decision matters to the user.
- Name the alternative when it reveals a meaningful tradeoff.
- Keep commentary brief enough to continue designing.

Use this speaking pattern:

**Decision → reason → tradeoff**

**Say:**

“I’m choosing ___ because ___. The tradeoff is ___.”

### 4. Start with the user’s job, not a familiar screen

Before choosing an interface, identify:

- What is the person trying to accomplish?
- What makes the situation difficult today?
- What decision or action moves them forward?
- What context does the system need?
- What could go wrong?
- What should success feel like?

### 5. Design the relationship, not only the interface

For a product that may cross hardware, software, and AI, consider:

- What the person is doing physically.
- What has their attention.
- What the system can perceive or remember.
- What the system understands versus infers.
- How it communicates its understanding.
- When it suggests, asks, or acts.
- How the person corrects, interrupts, or reverses it.

Do not assume every solution needs a conventional app, dashboard, or chatbot.

### 6. Scope aggressively

In one hour, aim for:

- One primary user.
- One important moment.
- One coherent core path.
- One consequential uncertainty, failure, or recovery state.
- One clear statement about what is deferred.

**Say:**

“There are several valuable directions. I’m focusing on ___ because it contains the most important user decision and the riskiest product assumption.”

### 7. Use roughness intentionally

- Make the logic visible before polishing the visuals.
- Begin with short labels, boxes, arrows, and state changes.
- Bring one critical interaction to a convincing level of craft.
- Do not spend the first half of the session perfecting components.

### 8. Collaborate without constantly seeking approval

- Invite input at decision points, not after every move.
- Offer a point of view before asking for a reaction.
- Treat feedback as new product information.
- Change direction without apologizing or becoming defensive.

**Say:**

“I see two directions. My instinct is ___ because ___. Does that align with how you’re thinking about the problem?”

When challenged:

“That changes one of my assumptions. In that case, I’d adjust ___ because ___.”

### 9. Show ambition through the idea, not the amount of UI

- Propose a meaningful improvement to the relationship between the person and the system.
- Keep the idea grounded in a real behavior or need.
- Avoid novelty that cannot be explained or tested.
- Do not cram the hour with disconnected features.

### 10. Finish the story

Reserve time to explain:

- The user and situation.
- The friction.
- The chosen direction.
- The key interaction.
- The most important tradeoff.
- The riskiest assumption.
- What I would test next.

## The exercise framework

Use this sequence for almost any prompt:

**Frame → Choose → Map → Make → Stress-test → Tell**

## 1. Frame the problem

### Goal

Turn the prompt into a precise human problem.

### Capture in Figma

Create a small framing area with:

- **User:** Who is experiencing the problem?
- **Situation:** When and where does it happen?
- **Job:** What are they trying to accomplish?
- **Friction:** What prevents them from doing it well today?
- **Outcome:** What should become easier, faster, safer, or more understandable?
- **Constraints:** What must the solution respect?
- **Assumptions:** What am I treating as true for this exercise?

### Problem statement

Use this structure:

“When **[situation]**, **[user]** needs to **[job]**, but **[friction]**. The opportunity is to help them **[desired outcome]** without **[important cost or risk]**.”

### Example of the thinking—not a Blackstar product assumption

“When moving between tasks, a person needs to recover the context of what they were doing, but that context is fragmented across tools. The opportunity is to help them resume with less reconstruction without making the system feel invasive or unpredictable.”

## 2. Choose the focus

### Goal

Show prioritization before execution.

### Generate directions quickly

Sketch two or three possible approaches as short concepts, not complete screens.

For each direction, consider:

- User value.
- Frequency or importance of the moment.
- Distinction from the current experience.
- Technical or behavioral risk.
- What the concept would help the team learn.

### Select one direction

Choose based on:

1. Does it address the central user job?
2. Does it expose the riskiest assumption?
3. Can I demonstrate it clearly within the available time?

### State the decision

“I considered ___ and ___. I’m choosing ___ because it most directly improves ___. I’m deferring ___ because it does not change the core interaction yet.”

## 3. Map the experience

### Goal

Understand the complete moment before detailing a screen.

Map the smallest useful flow:

**Trigger → system understanding → user decision → system response → outcome → recovery**

For a cross-device or ambient experience, add:

- Physical context.
- Input or signal.
- Where feedback appears.
- What happens when attention moves elsewhere.
- How the experience returns to a stable state.

### The system contract

Answer these questions:

| Question | Design responsibility |
|---|---|
| What does the system know? | Show relevant context without unnecessary exposure. |
| What is it inferring? | Distinguish interpretation from fact. |
| What does it want to do? | Make the next step understandable. |
| What is the consequence? | Match the level of confirmation to the level of risk. |
| What can the person control? | Allow editing, choosing, pausing, stopping, or declining. |
| How does it recover? | Support correction, retry, undo, or an understandable fallback. |

## 4. Make the critical interaction

### Goal

Turn the product idea into behavior someone can understand and react to.

Design the three essential moments:

1. **Before:** The trigger and context are understandable.
2. **During:** The system and person exchange the information needed to move forward.
3. **After:** The outcome, status, or next step is clear.

### Craft priority

Spend visual-design time on the moment carrying the most meaning, risk, or emotional weight.

Check:

- Is the primary action obvious?
- Is the information hierarchy clear?
- Does the interaction reveal the system’s state?
- Is feedback timely and located where attention already is?
- Does the interface ask only for information the system cannot reasonably infer?
- Does the experience feel calm, intentional, and physically plausible?

### Figma working method

- Use one page unless the prompt requires more.
- Keep the prompt, assumptions, flow, and screens visible on the canvas.
- Use frames and auto layout for speed.
- Create components only for elements that meaningfully repeat.
- Use short annotations to explain behavior that a static frame cannot show.
- Connect only the key prototype path.
- Keep exploratory work visible unless it becomes distracting.

Suggested canvas organization:

1. **Prompt and framing**
2. **Directions considered**
3. **Core flow**
4. **Key interaction**
5. **Important state**
6. **Tradeoffs and next test**

## 5. Stress-test the idea

### Goal

Show that the design survives contact with uncertainty and consequence.

Choose the one state most likely to reveal whether the concept is trustworthy:

- The system misunderstands intent.
- Context or input is missing.
- The person changes their mind.
- The system needs permission.
- An action is consequential or irreversible.
- A device or service is unavailable.
- The person is interrupted.
- The system is uncertain.
- The action only partially succeeds.

Ask:

- What does the person believe is happening?
- What is actually happening?
- How does the product close that gap?
- Can the person recover without starting over?

### Blackstar lens

For a new computing interaction, specifically examine:

- **Attention:** Does this deserve interruption now?
- **Context:** Is the system using the right information?
- **Agency:** Can the person understand and change what happens?
- **Privacy:** Is sensitive context visible or used appropriately?
- **Continuity:** Does the experience survive movement across moments or surfaces?
- **Physical reality:** Does the behavior make sense in the person’s environment?

## 6. Tell the story

### Goal

Make the quality of the thinking easy to understand.

### Two-minute final walkthrough

“I focused on **[user]** in **[situation]**. They are trying to **[job]**, but **[friction]** makes that difficult.”

“I considered **[alternatives]** and chose **[direction]** because **[reason]**.”

“The experience works by **[short before/during/after explanation]**.”

“The key design decision is **[decision]**. The tradeoff is **[tradeoff]**.”

“The riskiest assumption is **[assumption]**. The first thing I would test is **[test]**, using **[signal of success]**.”

## The 60-minute plan

Treat these as checkpoints, not rigid ceremonies.

| Time | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 min | Listen and restate | Shared understanding of the prompt |
| 5–10 min | Frame | User, job, friction, outcome, assumptions, constraints |
| 10–15 min | Choose | Two or three directions and one explicit decision |
| 15–22 min | Map | Small end-to-end flow and system contract |
| 22–42 min | Make | Core path and one resolved key interaction |
| 42–50 min | Stress-test | One meaningful uncertainty, failure, or recovery state |
| 50–55 min | Refine | Hierarchy, labels, consistency, and prototype connection |
| 55–60 min | Tell | Final walkthrough, tradeoff, next test, and discussion |

### Time-management rules

- By minute 10, have a problem statement.
- By minute 15, commit to a direction.
- By minute 22, have a visible flow.
- By minute 35, have the main experience on the canvas.
- At minute 42, stop expanding scope.
- Protect the final five minutes.
- If behind, reduce screen count before removing the core decision or recovery state.

### What to say when time is tight

“To protect the core story, I’m going to keep this state at a lower fidelity and spend the remaining time resolving ___.”

## High-leverage clarification questions

Ask three to five, not all of them.

### User and moment

- “Who is the primary person we are designing for?”
- “What are they trying to accomplish in this moment?”
- “How frequently or urgently does this happen?”
- “What is the most painful part of the current experience?”

### Product and environment

- “Should I assume a particular device, surface, or environment?”
- “What capabilities already exist, and what should I treat as open?”
- “Does this need to fit an existing product model, or can I explore a new one?”
- “Is there an important technical, privacy, or business constraint I should honor?”

### Success and scope

- “What outcome matters most: speed, confidence, completion, learning, or something else?”
- “Would you rather see breadth across the journey or depth in the critical interaction?”
- “Is there a particular part of my process you would like me to make especially visible?”

## Natural phrases to use

### Restating the problem

- “Let me play back what I heard.”
- “The user’s core job seems to be ___.”
- “The important tension is ___ versus ___.”

### Making assumptions

- “I don’t have that information, so I’ll assume ___ and mark it here.”
- “That assumption matters because it changes ___.”

### Choosing a direction

- “There are a few plausible directions.”
- “I’m choosing this one because it addresses the critical moment most directly.”
- “I’m intentionally leaving ___ outside the first version.”

### Explaining a decision

- “The reason this is visible is ___.”
- “I’m asking rather than acting automatically because ___.”
- “This is where the person needs confidence, not just speed.”
- “The tradeoff is ___, and I’m comfortable with it here because ___.”

### Inviting collaboration

- “My current point of view is ___. What reaction does that bring up for you?”
- “Does that match the constraint you had in mind?”
- “I can go deeper into the system behavior or the visual interaction—which would be more useful?”

### Responding to feedback

- “That changes the problem in an important way.”
- “I agree that this part is doing too much. I’d simplify it by ___.”
- “I see the concern. Another option is ___, but its tradeoff would be ___.”

### Closing

- “The riskiest assumption is ___.”
- “The first thing I would test is ___.”
- “With more time, I would validate ___ before expanding the feature set.”

## Decision lenses

When choosing between two designs, compare them through the lenses that matter to the problem.

| Lens | Question |
|---|---|
| Usefulness | Does this help the person complete the real job? |
| Clarity | Can they understand what is happening and why? |
| Effort | How much attention, input, or memory does it demand? |
| Agency | Can they shape, decline, interrupt, or reverse it? |
| Trust | Are context, uncertainty, and consequences legible? |
| Continuity | Does it work across time, states, or surfaces? |
| Feasibility | What technical assumption does it depend on? |
| Learning | What would this concept allow the team to test? |

Do not score every lens mechanically. Use the few that expose the real tradeoff.

## If the prompt involves AI

Do not begin with a chatbot. Define the AI’s job first.

### AI role

- What can AI do better than a deterministic interface here?
- What context does it require?
- What part is fact, inference, or recommendation?
- When should it ask a clarifying question?
- When should it remain passive?
- What consequence requires explicit approval?

### Interaction choice

Choose the form based on the user’s need:

- **Natural language:** Flexible intent, exploration, explanation.
- **Structured controls:** Precision, repeatability, comparison, configuration.
- **Visualization:** Patterns, relationships, change over time.
- **Ambient signal:** Awareness without demanding full attention.
- **Direct manipulation:** Fast, concrete control over an object or state.
- **Combination:** Use only when each form has a distinct job.

### Trust contract

The person should be able to understand:

1. What context the system used.
2. What it understood or inferred.
3. What it proposes to do.
4. What will change.
5. What still requires human judgment.
6. How to correct or recover.

## What not to do

- Do not start drawing before establishing the user and problem.
- Do not spend fifteen minutes asking questions.
- Do not narrate every cursor movement.
- Do not give a generic speech about the double diamond or design process.
- Do not polish the first frame before mapping the experience.
- Do not produce many disconnected screens.
- Do not hide uncertainty or pretend an assumption is a fact.
- Do not ask “Is this right?” after every decision.
- Do not defend the first idea when new evidence appears.
- Do not treat AI as a generic chat panel.
- Do not limit the solution to a screen when the context suggests physical or ambient behavior.
- Do not sacrifice the final explanation for one more component.

## Final self-review

### Problem

- [ ] I can name the primary user, situation, job, and friction in one sentence.
- [ ] My assumptions and constraints are visible.
- [ ] I defined a user outcome rather than only a feature.

### Product judgment

- [ ] I considered more than one direction.
- [ ] I explained why I chose this direction.
- [ ] I named what I intentionally deferred.
- [ ] The concept addresses the riskiest part of the problem.

### Interaction

- [ ] The before, during, and after states form one coherent story.
- [ ] The primary decision or action is clear.
- [ ] System status and feedback are understandable.
- [ ] I included one meaningful uncertainty, failure, or recovery state.

### Blackstar relevance

- [ ] I considered context and attention, not only screen layout.
- [ ] I considered the relationship between physical behavior and software response.
- [ ] The person retains appropriate agency.
- [ ] Privacy and consequences are proportional to the concept.
- [ ] The idea is ambitious but grounded in a real human need.

### Communication

- [ ] I explained decisions and tradeoffs instead of cursor movements.
- [ ] I invited collaboration at meaningful moments.
- [ ] I responded to feedback without defensiveness.
- [ ] I protected time for the final walkthrough.

### Validation

- [ ] I identified the riskiest assumption.
- [ ] I named the first test I would run.
- [ ] I defined what I would observe or measure.

## Practice prompts

These are practice scenarios, not assumptions about Blackstar’s product.

### Prompt 1 — Recover context

Design an experience that helps someone return to an interrupted task without manually reopening and reconstructing everything.

**Practice focus:** Context, continuity, privacy, and correction.

### Prompt 2 — Protect attention

Design how a personal computing system decides when and how to bring an important event to someone’s attention.

**Practice focus:** Urgency, interruption, physical environment, and control.

### Prompt 3 — Move from intent to action

Design an experience that helps someone express a goal and allows the system to complete work across several tools.

**Practice focus:** Intent, plan visibility, permission, progress, and recovery.

### Prompt 4 — Remember without becoming invasive

Design how a computing system helps someone retrieve something useful from an earlier moment.

**Practice focus:** Memory boundaries, provenance, sensitive context, and forgetting.

## Practice review rubric

Score each category from 1 to 5 after a timed run.

| Category | A strong run demonstrates |
|---|---|
| Framing | A precise user, situation, job, friction, and outcome |
| Prioritization | A clear choice and explicit deferred scope |
| Product thinking | A coherent idea grounded in user behavior |
| System thinking | Inputs, states, boundaries, consequences, and recovery |
| Interaction design | Clear actions, hierarchy, feedback, and transitions |
| Craft | One important moment resolved with care |
| Communication | Concise rationale, assumptions, and tradeoffs |
| Collaboration | Useful questions and receptive response to feedback |
| Time management | A finished core story and protected recap |
| Validation | A specific risk, test, and success signal |

After each practice, write only three notes:

1. **Keep:** What strengthened the solution?
2. **Change:** Where did I lose clarity or time?
3. **Next run:** What single behavior will I practice deliberately?
