## How I Want This To Sound

Normal and direct. More like explaining the work to onsite interviewers who may work on adjacent product areas, not giving a keynote or walking through a design-process checklist.

Use simple words. Say what happened, what I tried, what I changed, and why it mattered.

Opening frame for both projects:

> I will show two stages of the same AI product journey. Project 1 shows how I led a shipped assistant from low adoption to contextual workflows with measurable results. Project 2 shows how I used that foundation to define a new agentic product direction with product and engineering.

The main point:

> CHAI started as a help bot. It became useful when we stopped making admins figure out how to use the assistant, and started putting the right AI help into the places where admins were already working.

For Google AI Foundations, the point I want them to hear is:

> I was not just designing a chatbot. I was designing how people interact with complex product data through AI: how the product supplies context, routes to the right capability, explains limits, and turns data into usable outputs.

Positioning line to reinforce at the opening and close:

> I design trustworthy AI data tools that help technical users move from complex data to clear insight and confident action.

## What This Project Should Prove

- I can take an AI product with weak adoption and find the real workflow and data problem.
- I can design complex, information-dense enterprise tools without making them feel heavier.
- I can define conceptual models for AI-driven data tools: intent, data context, routing, tool use, output format, guardrails, and reviewable outputs.
- I can design human-in-the-loop clarification moments that improve AI accuracy without turning the flow into a form.
- I can create reusable patterns for AI answers instead of designing one-off screens.
- I can work with research and domain experts to model an unfamiliar technical workflow before designing the interface.
- I can turn fragmented diagnostic data into evidence users can inspect, challenge, and share.
- I can design the full path from a user's question to scoped data, structured analysis, evidence, a decision-ready report, and a durable trace.
- I can partner through research, PM, engineering, and design-system constraints from ideation to execution.
- I can connect product quality to measurable behavior: adoption, search success, and real workflow entry points.

## The Shape Of The Story

This should not feel like a feature tour. The middle of the story should be about design exploration and design decisions:

1. CHAI launched as a help bot, but adoption was low.
2. Making the chat better helped, but did not solve the real problem.
3. The real problem was context and routing: different admin jobs needed different data, tools, analysis, and UI outputs.
4. I changed the model from "user manages assistant context" to "product supplies context."
5. If the user did not pick a skill, CHAI still used lightweight clarification prompts instead of guessing silently.
6. I built a pattern across Search, Reports, Analytics, Workspaces, and Devices.
7. Troubleshooting tested the model against the workflow users wanted most: fragmented diagnosis across multiple data surfaces.
8. The strongest proof is not only the entry points. It is the AI workflow system behind them: intent -> clarification/context -> skill/tool -> analysis/evidence -> UI output -> guardrails.
9. Guardrails and evidence-based widgets show my expertise in designing AI behavior for complex workflows, not just AI screens.

Plain line to remember:

> The entry point should already carry context.

Sharper role-specific line:

> For AI in complex tools, the design problem is not only the answer. It is how the product frames the question, supplies the context, exposes the system's limits, and makes the output usable.

## Expanded Talk Track And Q&A Bank

The sections below preserve the fuller story. Do not present every section in the 15-minute version. Use the slide plan above as the main talk track and pull details from this bank when an interviewer asks about process, iterations, metrics, edge cases, or collaboration.

### 1. Control Hub AI Assistant

Say:

> I will start with Control Hub AI Assistant, or CHAI. It is an AI assistant inside Webex Control Hub. I was the design lead on the assistant experience, the entry points, the skill model, and how CHAI showed up across admin workflows.

Then:

> The reason I want to show this project is not just that it had metrics. It shows how I think about AI inside a complex product: where the AI should show up, what context it should use, what it should expose, and how the output becomes useful inside the user's workflow.

Keep this short. Do not over-explain the org or team.

Plain line:

> This project was about turning a help bot into a useful AI layer inside work.

### 2. What Is Control Hub?

Say:

> Before I show CHAI, I need to explain Control Hub. Control Hub is the admin console for Webex. It is where admins configure services, look at usage, manage devices, troubleshoot issues, and keep everything running.

Then:

> The problem is not that there is no information. The problem is that there is a lot of information. On a bad day, an admin is looking at dashboards, settings, reports, device status, alerts, and support signals, and trying to figure out what actually matters.

Point to:

- The left navigation.
- The number of product areas.
- Analytics and monitoring.
- Reports.
- Devices and workspaces.

Plain line:

> Their problem was density, not lack of information.

### 3. The Week After Launch

Say:

> The first version of CHAI launched as a help bot. The week after launch, adoption was low: around 3% weekly adoption. Most admins tried it once and never came back.

Then:

> That was interesting because CHAI was not simply giving terrible answers. It could answer a question like, "How do I configure SSO?" and give a reasonable step-by-step response. But it still felt separate from the work. The admin had to stop, open CHAI, figure out what to ask, and connect the answer back to whatever they were doing.

The point:

- CHAI v1 answered questions.
- Weekly adoption was low.
- Users did not build a habit around it.
- It relied too much on the user knowing the right prompt.
- It felt like help content beside the product, not help inside the product.

Plain line:

> It was correct enough to try once, but not useful enough to come back to.

### 4. Better Chat Was Not Enough

Say:

> The first exploration was the obvious one: can we make the chat itself better?

Show:

- Better empty state.
- Better suggested prompts.
- Thread history.
- Cleaner answer layout.
- More consistent assistant behavior.

Then:

> These changes mattered. They made CHAI more usable. But they also showed me the limit. It was still not serving what users actually needed in the moment. Even with a better conversation, admins still had to know when to open it, what to ask, and how to connect the answer back to the page they were on.

Plain line:

> Better chat was useful, but it was not the full answer.

### 5. Research Turned Needs Into Roadmap

Say:

> To understand what was missing, I talked to customers and looked at where admins actually wanted AI to help in Control Hub. They were not only asking for a better chat panel. They wanted help when they were trying to find something, understand data, or fix an issue.

Then:

> That became the roadmap: Find, Understand, and Fix. It gave us a clearer product direction, but it also exposed the next design problem: each of those jobs needed different context and different tools.

Then:

> That is where routing became the real design problem. A help question, a search question, a report question, a data question, and a workspace troubleshooting question all need different context and different tools. If CHAI routes the question wrong, the answer becomes generic or inaccurate.

What this slide should explain:

- I used customer conversations to understand where AI could help in real workflows.
- CHAI was expanding from one assistant into multiple capabilities.
- Find, Understand, and Fix came from user needs, not just internal feature planning.
- Each capability needed different context and tools.
- Better answers depended on better context.
- Admins should not have to manage routing manually.

Plain line:

> The assistant was getting more powerful, but also harder to explain.

### 6. Decision 1: Turn Routing Into Skills

Say:

> The early version exposed routing too directly. Users had to do something like `@Add context` to tell CHAI what it should use. That made sense technically, but it was not how admins thought about their work. They were not thinking, "I need to set assistant context." They were thinking, "I need help with this report," or "I need to understand this workspace issue."

Decision:

> So I changed the model from context-setting to Skills. Instead of asking users to route the assistant, CHAI showed what it could help with. When a user selected a skill, the welcome page changed: the intro text, suggested prompts, and next steps all became relevant to that skill.

How I would show it:

- Before: `@Add context` asks the user to understand the assistant's backend need.
- After: Skills explain the capability in user language.
- Skill selection sets the right context behind the scenes.
- The welcome page becomes dynamic based on the selected skill.
- Suggested prompts teach users what CHAI can do in that mode.

Why it mattered:

- It educated users without a tutorial.
- It made CHAI's capabilities easier to scan.
- It routed users to the right context without asking them to manage context.
- It supported better tool use, data access, and answer accuracy.

Plain line:

> I turned a technical routing problem into a user-facing skill model.

### 7. Decision 1B: Clarify Before Guessing

Say:

> Skills were the proactive routing model. But I also knew not every admin would choose a skill first. Some users will just type a natural question into the assistant.

Then:

> In those cases, I did not want CHAI to guess silently. If the user asked something ambiguous, CHAI could ask one lightweight clarification question. That kept the human in the loop and helped the assistant choose the right signal, scope, or tool.

Use the attached design as the direction, but make the example sharper:

User asks:

> Why are my workspaces underused this week?

CHAI asks:

> I can analyze "underused" in a few ways. Which signal should I use?

Options:

- Actual people presence from workspace devices.
- Scheduled room usage from calendars and meetings.
- Compare both, and flag rooms that were booked but empty.
- Let me define it another way.

Why this is better than a generic clarification:

- It explains why CHAI needs the clarification.
- It uses admin language, not model language.
- It gives a recommended richer path: compare both.
- It turns ambiguity into a useful analysis direction.
- It teaches the user what signals Control Hub can use.

Plain line:

> Clarification is not friction if it prevents the assistant from making a hidden assumption.

### 8. AI Workflow Architecture

Say:

> This is the system behind the screens. For each CHAI moment, I was thinking about six pieces: what the user is trying to do, what data context the product already has, which CHAI skill or tool should handle it, what analysis or evidence is needed, what UI output is actually useful, and what guardrails are needed when the system is uncertain.

Show the model:

- User intent.
- Product context.
- CHAI skill or tool.
- Analysis and evidence.
- UI output.
- Guardrails.

Then:

> This mattered because not every AI answer should look like chat. A search answer, a report analysis, a dashboard insight, and a device troubleshooting answer need different evidence and different UI. The design system had to make room for that.

For Google AI Foundations:

> This is the part I would connect to AI Foundations. If an AI data tool is helping people work across large systems, the design has to make intent, available data, source scope, tool choice, evidence, and output format understandable.

Plain line:

> I was designing the AI workflow model, not just the chat surface.

### 9. Decision 2: Product Supplies The Context

Say:

> Once the skill model was clearer, the next decision was where CHAI should show up. We explored global assistant entry, page-level triggers, inline prompts, search-triggered prompts, report rows, dashboards, workspaces, and device pages.

Then:

> The decision was to meet users where they already were. If someone is searching, CHAI should understand the search. If someone is looking at a report, CHAI should understand the report. If someone is troubleshooting a workspace or device, CHAI should stay close to that evidence.

The point:

- The assistant should not rely on the user to describe everything.
- The product already knows where the user is.
- The entry point can carry context.
- Context makes the AI output more specific and easier to trust.

Plain line:

> The product should supply the context before the user has to type.

### 10. Smart Search

Say:

> Smart Search is the clearest example of meeting the user where they are. When admins did not know where something lived, they already went to search. So instead of asking them to open CHAI, I put CHAI into that moment.

Then:

> The key design choice was the handoff. If the admin searched for something and needed more help, we showed useful follow-up questions right there. When they clicked one, CHAI opened with the search context still attached.

Call out:

- The query was preserved.
- The follow-up questions were tied to the search.
- The assistant opened with context.
- Search stopped being a dead end.

Metrics:

- 86% drop in zero-result searches.
- 14% of total assistant entry points came from Smart Search.

Plain line:

> Smart Search worked because it fit into behavior admins already had.

### 11. Reports: Complex Workflow Pain

Say:

> Reports were a stronger test because the workflow was more complex. Admins were not just asking a help question. They were trying to understand data: generate a report, download it, inspect rows, compare metrics, and figure out what actually mattered.

Then:

> That made the design problem different. A generic chat answer was not enough. CHAI needed to know which report the admin meant, what data was available, what question they were asking, and what output would actually help them make a decision.

What this should show:

- Reports were information-dense.
- The old flow required too much manual interpretation.
- The AI entry point needed to be close to the data.
- The output needed structure, not just prose.

Plain line:

> In reports, the hard part was turning data into a useful question and a usable answer.

### 12. Pattern Evolution: Row -> Page -> Dashboard

Say:

> The report pattern evolved as the system got better at handling context. At first, CHAI could use one report as context, so we put the entry point directly on the report row. That made the scope clear: CHAI is going to use this report.

Then:

> As the capability improved, users could ask questions across reports, so the pattern moved to `Ask AI` on the report page. Then the same pattern extended to analytics dashboards, where admins could ask about dashboard data and trends.

Show the pattern evolution:

- Report row sparkle: one report as context.
- `Ask AI` on Reports: questions across reports.
- `Ask AI` on Analytics Dashboards: questions about dashboard data and trends.

Why this matters:

- The UI evolved with the capability.
- The pattern stayed consistent.
- The entry point always communicated scope.
- The same contextual AI model scaled across surfaces.

Plain line:

> The pattern grew as the system got better at handling context.

### 13. Designing AI Limits

Say:

> One of the most important parts of designing AI in complex tools is making its limits visible. The happy path is not enough. If the data is missing or stale, a source is unavailable or outside the user's access, or the evidence does not support a conclusion, the user still needs a useful next step.

Then:

> I designed these states around a simple structure: what happened, what data or access condition caused it, and what the admin can do next.

Examples:

- Data is missing, delayed, or stale.
- A required source is unavailable.
- The user does not have access to a source.
- The question is too ambiguous to choose the right metric or scope.
- The available evidence does not support a reliable conclusion.
- A transformation or visualization cannot be completed.

Why this matters:

- It makes limitations visible.
- It distinguishes a data-quality problem from a model or access problem.
- It keeps trust when the answer is imperfect.
- It avoids dead ends.
- It teaches users how to ask better questions without sounding like an error message.

Plain line:

> For AI in complex tools, failure states are part of the product.

### 14. AI-Generated Reports

Say:

> AI-generated reports were the next step in that same workflow. The old flow was: generate a report, check the data, then ask CHAI what the report means.

Then:

> I wanted to flip that. Instead of starting with a form and ending with analysis, the admin could start with the question they actually had. CHAI could help generate a custom report with the metrics, filters, and insights they needed.

Design decision:

> The important design choice was to keep the output reviewable. Natural language reduced the setup work, but it did not replace structured configuration. The admin still needed to see and change the data sources, time range, metrics, filters, schedule, assumptions, and layout before trusting the result.

Lifecycle to show:

1. The admin starts with the decision or question.
2. CHAI interprets likely data sources, time range, metrics, and filters.
3. The admin resolves ambiguity or adjusts the scope.
4. CHAI proposes a report structure and initial interpretation.
5. The report exposes sources, freshness, applied filters or transformations, assumptions, and unsupported gaps.
6. The admin edits the configuration, regenerates one section, or compares alternatives.
7. The report can be saved, shared, exported, or scheduled.
8. Later viewers can inspect the report configuration and change history.

What to show:

- The user's question.
- CHAI's interpretation of sources, scope, metrics, and filters.
- The generated report structure.
- Key insights.
- Sources, freshness, transformations, assumptions, and unsupported gaps.
- The review/edit/regenerate moment.
- Save, share, export, schedule, and history.

Plain line:

> We moved from "make a report, then ask for insight" to "ask for the insight, then generate the report around it."

Tradeoff line:

> I used natural language for intent and structured controls for precision. The AI accelerated report creation, but the user still owned the report definition.

### 15. Troubleshooting Was The Number-One Ask

Say:

> After helping admins find information and understand data, the next problem was troubleshooting. It was the number-one capability users wanted from AI, but the current workflow was fragmented across five or more screens. Admins had to connect health data, meeting or calling details, device signals, logs, and support knowledge on their own.

Then:

> I did not want to design that workflow from assumptions. I partnered with our researcher to run a workshop with experts from Cisco's Technical Assistance Center, or TAC. We used real troubleshooting patterns to learn what they look for, where they lose time, and whether our early ideas matched how diagnosis actually works.

Three insights:

1. Diagnosis was spread across five or more screens with no connective tissue.
2. Many tickets were not deep technical failures. They were knowledge gaps or configuration issues.
3. The data existed, but it was buried in formats that were difficult to connect and interpret.

Why this matters:

- It shows the problem came from user demand and expert research, not an AI feature idea.
- It explains why better chat alone would not solve troubleshooting.
- It sets up the need for a model that connects signals across surfaces.

Plain line:

> The information existed. The missing piece was a way to connect it into a diagnosis.

### 16. Map The Diagnostic Logic First

Say:

> Before I designed the UI, I mapped the logic of the workflow because not every support case follows the same path. Sometimes the issue affects one user and sometimes many. Sometimes the pattern is obvious and sometimes it is intermittent. Meetings and Calling also expose different signals and require different diagnostic paths.

Then:

> Mapping those branches changed how I thought about the product. This was not just a chat interface that returned an answer. It was a decision-support system that needed to determine scope, gather the right evidence, compare signals, and help the admin decide what to do next.

What to show in the model:

- Scope: one user, a group, a location, or a broader service issue.
- Domain: Meetings, Calling, devices, or configuration.
- Pattern: isolated, repeated, intermittent, or widespread.
- Issue type: knowledge gap, configuration problem, or technical failure.
- Outcome: explain, recommend a change, gather more evidence, or escalate.

Design decision:

> I designed the diagnostic logic before designing the response.

### 17. Build A Case, Not Just An Answer

Say:

> I did not want the assistant to jump straight to a conclusion. I wanted it to build a case. Instead of only stating a root cause, the troubleshooting response shows what happened, when it happened, what evidence supports that reading, what might not fit, and what the next step could be.

Then:

> This is where the widget system became useful. Raw logs and telemetry were translated into structured evidence that an admin could scan and challenge: an incident summary, timeline, affected scope, source and freshness, supporting signals, conflicting signals, likely explanation, assumptions or missing evidence, and recommended action.

Show one concrete example as a progression:
