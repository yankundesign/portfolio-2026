# Salesforce Interview 1 — AI Fluency, Team Working Style, and Accountability

**Interviewer:** Kent Allison, Director of Product Management  
**Competency:** Take Accountability

## What Kent Is Likely Testing

- Can I use AI with real product judgment, not just talk about tools?
- Can I own a complex initiative from problem framing through measurable outcome?
- Can I keep PM, engineering, research, and design aligned over time?
- Do I make priorities, owners, progress, risks, and decisions visible?
- Can I admit when the first approach is not working and change direction?

## Core Message

> I use AI to make complex enterprise work more understandable and actionable. My working style is collaborative, but I create clear ownership around the user problem, decisions, next steps, and measures of success.

## Story Bank

- **CHAI adoption turnaround:** Long-term ownership, measurable progress, changing an ineffective direction.
- **Control Hub Agentic:** AI fluency, ambiguity, cross-functional alignment, trust and accountability.
- **Smart Search:** Evidence-based iteration and a concrete customer outcome.
- **SAP Fieldglass:** Cross-functional planning and scalable enterprise execution.

## Q&A

### 1. How would you describe your AI fluency as a product designer?

- **Testing:** Practical AI knowledge and product judgment.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I have designed AI products at three levels: conversational assistance, contextual AI inside workflows, and agentic workflows that can plan and act."
  - "With CHAI, I worked on search, report analysis, dashboards, and troubleshooting, so I had to understand context, routing, grounding, and failure states."
  - "For agentic work, I defined the contract around intent, context, plan, permission, execution, and audit."
  - "I also use tools like Cursor, Codex, and Figma Make to build working prototypes, but I keep product judgment in my hands."
  - "For me, AI fluency means understanding both what the system can do and what the user must see to trust it."

### 2. Tell me about an AI product you owned end to end.

- **Testing:** Ownership, AI judgment, and measurable results.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "CHAI launched in Webex Control Hub as a help chatbot, but monthly adoption was only 3%."
  - **Task:** "As the sole design lead, I needed to determine why admins were not returning and turn the assistant into something useful in daily work."
  - **Action:** "I improved the conversation experience, studied where admins actually needed help, and reframed the roadmap around finding information, understanding data, and fixing problems."
  - **Action:** "I then designed Skills and contextual entry points across search, reports, dashboards, workspaces, and devices."
  - **Result:** "Monthly adoption grew from 3% to 18%, and Smart Search reduced dead-end searches by 86%."
  - **Learning:** "The real problem was not chat quality alone. It was whether AI appeared with the right context at the right moment."

### 3. How have you ensured that you and others shared responsibility for a long-term objective?

- **Testing:** Take Accountability — focus, measures, and shared ownership.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "CHAI was expanding from one help experience into search, reports, analytics, and device workflows."
  - **Task:** "I needed to keep the work coherent across multiple partners and releases while improving adoption."
  - **Action:** "I gave the roadmap three stable user jobs—Find, Understand, and Fix—so every proposed capability had to support a clear user outcome."
  - **Action:** "With PM, I clarified priorities; with engineering and data partners, I worked through available context and reliability; with research, I checked whether the interaction matched admin behavior."
  - **Action:** "I used adoption, search success, and workflow entry points as progress signals, while design reviews made decisions and open risks visible."
  - **Result:** "The team could expand CHAI without turning it into disconnected features, and adoption moved from 3% to 18%."

### 4. Describe a difficult initiative you were responsible for. How did you achieve the objective?

- **Testing:** Persistence, prioritization, and accountability under complexity.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "The team needed a north star for agentic AI in Control Hub, but there was no established interaction model for how admins would access, manage, and work with agents."
  - **Task:** "Using the initial use cases provided by PM, I was responsible for defining the interaction framework and turning those separate scenarios into one coherent agent experience."
  - **Action:** "I created the agent framework for how admins discover and manage agents, start work, review plans, and follow progress across Control Hub."
  - **Action:** "I defined a plan-first model with visible execution and expanded agent-action details in the Control Hub audit model, so users could understand what the agent proposed, what they approved, and what changed. I also worked across Cisco teams to align on shared design patterns."
  - **Action:** "I built a working React prototype so product, design, and engineering could evaluate real states, handoffs, approvals, and audit details instead of reviewing disconnected screens."
  - **Result:** "The prototype aligned stakeholders around a concrete direction and gained buy-in for the experience to be demonstrated at Cisco Live US 2026."

### 5. How do you plan a complex initiative and decide who does what by when?

- **Testing:** Deliberate planning and ownership clarity.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I start by defining the user outcome and the few decisions that can block the work."
  - "Then I map dependencies: research questions, product decisions, technical feasibility, data availability, interaction states, and validation."
  - "I make the owner and next decision clear for each dependency instead of treating everything as a shared task."
  - "I use prototypes and regular reviews as checkpoints because they reveal uncertainty earlier than status updates alone."
  - "If something is at risk, I surface the tradeoff early: reduce scope, change sequence, or get the missing owner involved."

### 6. Tell me about a time your first approach was not working.

- **Testing:** Accountability without defensiveness.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "The first phase of CHAI focused on making the chatbot easier to use."
  - **Task:** "I needed to improve adoption, not simply deliver a cleaner chat experience."
  - **Action:** "I improved prompts, threads, and answer layouts, but I paid attention when those changes did not solve the habit problem."
  - **Action:** "I acknowledged that our product model was incomplete and shifted the work toward contextual entry points inside existing admin workflows."
  - **Result:** "That reframing led to Skills, Smart Search, report analysis, and ultimately the adoption increase from 3% to 18%."
  - **Learning:** "Accountability means staying committed to the outcome, not defending the first solution."

### 7. What is your preferred working style with product management?

- **Testing:** PM partnership and constructive challenge.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I work best with PM as a close product partner, with shared ownership of the customer outcome and clear ownership of our different contributions."
  - "I look to PM for business context, priorities, and constraints, while I bring user evidence, workflow understanding, and experience quality into the product decision."
  - "I prefer design to be involved while we are defining the problem and success measures, not only after the feature and scope are already fixed."
  - "In a multi-product environment, I like a visible prioritization framework so the designer is not simply reacting to the loudest request from several PMs."
  - "When we disagree, I make the assumptions and tradeoffs concrete, test what we can, and fully support the decision once we align."
- **If they ask about a PM-heavy organization:** "I am comfortable working with strong PM leadership. The partnership works best when PM and design can challenge each other, customer evidence has a real place in roadmap decisions, and design has enough continuity to protect the end-to-end experience across product areas."

### 8. Tell me about a disagreement with a PM.

- **Testing:** Product judgment, trust, and commitment after disagreement.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "An early CHAI direction treated low adoption mainly as a need to improve the chat experience."
  - **Task:** "I needed to show that the larger problem was workflow fit, without turning it into a debate about design preference."
  - **Action:** "I separated the symptom from the cause and brought in admin behavior: users had to leave their task, open CHAI, invent a prompt, and reconnect the answer to their work."
  - **Action:** "I showed contextual alternatives in search, reports, dashboards, and device pages so the team could compare the models concretely."
  - **Result:** "We aligned on contextual AI as the broader direction, which contributed to adoption growing from 3% to 18%."
  - **Closer:** "I try to disagree by reframing the user problem, not by arguing taste."

## Three Backup PM-Disagreement Stories

### Backup 1 — SAP Fieldglass: Improve Search or Redesign the Dashboard

- **Best for:** Customer focus, influence, research, and a shipped result.
- **Confidence:** Fully supported by the portfolio.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "At SAP Fieldglass, the initial roadmap asks included improving Global Search and updating the homepage during the UI5 migration."
  - **Task:** "I disagreed that better search alone would solve the main problem for hiring managers, because many were occasional users who did not know what to search for in the first place."
  - **Action:** "I brought research and persona evidence to the PM and engineering lead, but I also acknowledged the backend cost and the reason search was attractive as a contained scope."
  - **Action:** "I proposed a dashboard alternative with role-based templates, visible work items, worker signals, and in-place actions, then mapped four personas to the jobs the default experience needed to support."
  - **Result:** "We aligned on the broader dashboard direction, and the modular homepage framework was adopted by more than 1,000 enterprise customers."
  - **Closer:** "I did not reject the original request; I connected it to the deeper customer problem and offered a scalable alternative."
- **If they ask how you handled the disagreement:** "I made sure the PM could see that the proposal still met the migration and delivery goals, rather than asking the team to accept a larger vision without a feasible path."

### Backup 2 — CHAI: Manual Context Selection or User-Facing Skills

- **Best for:** Technical workflows, simplifying platform complexity, and influencing through prototypes.
- **Confirm before using:** Was the PM specifically advocating for the `@Add context` model or asking to preserve explicit context selection?
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "As CHAI expanded into search, reports, analytics, and troubleshooting, the early direction asked users to manually add context so the system would know which tools and data to use."
  - **Task:** "I disagreed with exposing that routing model because it was technically clear but forced admins to understand how the assistant worked internally."
  - **Action:** "I first clarified the PM's concern: explicit context selection gave the team predictability and reduced incorrect routing."
  - **Action:** "I then prototyped a Skills model that described capabilities in admin language while selecting context and tools behind the scenes, with clarification prompts when the request was still ambiguous."
  - **Result:** "We moved from asking users to operate the routing system to a capability model that could scale across CHAI's different workflows."
  - **Closer:** "The design respected the technical need for routing without making the customer manage the architecture."
- **If the PM detail is not true:** Use this for "Tell me about balancing user needs and technical constraints," not for a PM disagreement.

### Backup 3 — Agentic Control Hub: New Activity System or Extend the Existing Audit Log

- **Best for:** Product partnership, customer trust, technical constraints, and finding common ground.
- **Answer to say (STAR):**
  - **Situation:** "In the agentic Control Hub project, I proposed a detailed Activity experience that would show what the agent did, what the admin approved, which resources changed, and the final result."
  - **Task:** "The PM disagreed with creating a separate experience and wanted to reuse the existing Control Hub audit log, even though its current structure could only show limited information about agent work."
  - **Action:** "I brought research showing that admins needed a more detailed record to trust agent actions. They wanted to understand the steps, approvals, systems touched, and resulting changes—not only see that an event occurred."
  - **Action:** "I worked with engineering to identify which agent-action details we could capture, and I worked with the PM on a compromise that preserved the existing audit-log structure."
  - **Result:** "We agreed to introduce a new audit-event type inside the existing system, with expanded details for agent actions, approvals, touched resources, and outcomes."
  - **Closer:** "I protected the customer need for transparency, while accepting the PM's point that we should extend a trusted platform pattern instead of creating a parallel system."
- **If they ask who changed their mind:** "We both adjusted. The PM agreed the existing level of detail was not enough for agent actions, and I agreed that the right solution was to evolve the existing audit log rather than replace it."

## Which Disagreement Story to Use

- **Safest, strongest PM story:** SAP Search versus role-based dashboard.
- **Best match for the Salesforce Platform role:** Manual context selection versus Skills, after confirming the PM detail.
- **Best example of productive compromise:** Detailed agent Activity versus extending the existing audit log.
- **Best story with a direct adoption metric:** The original CHAI chat-versus-contextual-workflows answer.

### 9. How do you keep a team accountable without formal authority?

- **Testing:** Senior influence and follow-through.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I create clarity before I create pressure. People need to know the decision, owner, dependency, and consequence of delay."
  - "I summarize decisions and next steps after reviews so responsibility does not disappear into a meeting."
  - "I make risks visible early and ask what support is missing before assuming a motivation problem."
  - "I also hold myself to the same standard: if design is blocking progress, I say what I will deliver and when."
  - "That consistency is usually more effective than escalating late."

### 10. How do you use AI in your own design process responsibly?

- **Testing:** AI working style, judgment, and quality control.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I use AI to increase the speed and fidelity of exploration, especially when interaction timing and state matter."
  - "For the agentic project, I used AI coding tools to build a working React prototype with plan, approval, execution, and Activity states."
  - "I do not delegate the product model to the tool. I define the behavior, review the output, and test edge cases."
  - "I am also careful about sensitive inputs and do not put customer or company-confidential information into tools without approval."
  - "The value is faster learning, not automated decision-making."

### 11. Tell me about your experience designing a complex technical product.

- **Testing:** Systems thinking, technical depth, and ability to simplify without losing meaning.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "Control Hub is a complex enterprise platform where IT admins manage services, devices, users, settings, analytics, and troubleshooting."
  - "The challenge was not a lack of information. It was that the relevant context was spread across dashboards, reports, settings, device state, and support signals."
  - "With CHAI, I designed how the product supplied context, routed requests to the right capability, exposed limitations, and turned technical data into reviewable outputs."
  - "I did not try to hide all the complexity. I made the current scope, evidence, system state, and available next action easier to understand."
  - "That approach helped monthly adoption grow from 3% to 18%, while Smart Search reduced dead-end searches by 86%."

### 12. How do you learn a complex technical domain when you are not the subject-matter expert?

- **Testing:** Learning process, humility, and effective use of domain experts.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I start by mapping the system, the user's goal, and the decisions they need to make—not by trying to memorize every technical term."
  - "I observe real workflows and artifacts such as logs, reports, support cases, and existing tools to understand where knowledge and context break down."
  - "For Control Hub troubleshooting, I partnered with research and Cisco Technical Assistance Center experts to learn how they determine scope, compare signals, and separate configuration issues from technical failures."
  - "I turn that learning into a workflow model and prototype, then bring it back to experts to find incorrect assumptions and missing edge cases."
  - "My role is not to pretend I am the expert; it is to make expert reasoning usable in the product."

### 13. How would you approach designing for technical users such as developers?

- **Testing:** Transferability to developer tools and respect for expert workflows.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "My most direct experience is with IT admins rather than software developers, but the design challenge is closely related: both groups work with complex systems, technical language, dependencies, and high-consequence actions."
  - "I would begin by understanding the developer's real workflow—what they are building, where they switch tools, what they need to inspect, and how they recover when something fails."
  - "I would preserve precision instead of translating everything into simplified product language. Technical users need clear system state, useful errors, inspectable inputs and outputs, and a path to deeper detail."
  - "I would partner closely with engineers and conduct research with developers at different experience levels so the product supports both a fast path and deeper control."
  - "The goal is not to make a technical tool feel basic. It is to make its complexity predictable and operable."
- **If they push on direct developer-tool experience:** "I would be transparent that this is an adjacent domain for me, then point to how I learned telecommunications, AI routing, analytics, and troubleshooting well enough to lead those experiences end to end."

### 14. How do you simplify a complex workflow without oversimplifying it?

- **Testing:** Product judgment and respect for expert users.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I separate complexity the user must understand from complexity the product can manage for them."
  - "In CHAI, admins did not need to manage the assistant's internal routing, so I translated that into user-facing Skills."
  - "But when the system was interpreting data or taking action, I preserved the evidence, scope, approval, execution state, and audit details because those affected trust."
  - "I use progressive disclosure so the common path stays focused while technical users can inspect details when they need them."
  - "Simplification should reduce unnecessary work, not remove the information required for a sound decision."

### 15. How do you prioritize requests from different PMs?

- **Testing:** Prioritization, transparency, and comfort in a multi-product organization.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I would not prioritize based on which PM asks first or pushes hardest. I would make the tradeoffs visible against a shared set of criteria."
  - "I look at customer and business impact, strategic alignment, urgency, dependencies, risk, and whether the work creates a reusable platform pattern."
  - "I clarify the real need and minimum useful outcome behind each request, because two large requests may have very different critical paths."
  - "Then I bring the comparison to the appropriate product and design leaders so the priority is an organizational decision, not a private negotiation between one designer and several PMs."
  - "Once we decide, I communicate what is now, next, and later—including what will move and why—so every PM has the same expectation."
- **If they ask about an urgent request:** "I would assess whether it changes the customer or business risk enough to reorder the work, then make the impact on existing commitments explicit before switching."

### 16. What would you do if a project had a very tight timeline?

- **Testing:** Speed, judgment, communication, and responsible scope management.
- **Answer to say:**
  - "I first align with PM and engineering on the outcome that must be true at launch, the critical user journey, and the highest-risk assumptions."
  - "I reduce scope before reducing quality: preserve core usability, accessibility, trust, and error handling, while deferring secondary paths or polish that does not affect the outcome."
  - "I prototype the riskiest interaction early, reuse proven design-system patterns, and work with engineering in parallel instead of waiting for a complete handoff."
  - "I make tradeoffs and unresolved risks visible throughout the project so no one discovers at the end that the timeline required a major compromise."
  - "If everything cannot fit, I propose a phased release with a coherent first version and clear follow-up work, rather than shipping an incomplete experience with no recovery plan."
- **If they ask what you would never cut:** "I would not cut the information users need to understand system state, recover from errors, or safely approve a consequential action."

- "What would make you say after six months that this designer has become a strong PM partner?"
